Santa 2025 Christmas Tree Packing Challenge

Kaggle competition (Google LLC) · November 2025 – February 2026

Silver Medal, top 3.3% of 3,357 teams. A team entry that I led.

Purpose
For every shipment size from 1 to 200, pack that many tree-shaped pieces into the smallest possible square box without overlaps. The score is the sum of each box's area divided by its number of trees, so every one of the 200 packings counts.
My role
My part: I led the team and built the Python pipeline below, from randomized starts through simulated annealing to greedy refinement. I ran all 200 puzzle sizes in parallel and asynchronously, and compared search settings across repeated runs.
Method
  1. Starting layouts, made two ways: random placement (adapted from Kaggle's starter notebook) and rows of alternating upright and upside-down trees that interlock.
  2. Keep the best: for each number of trees, keep the smallest layout from all candidate files.
  3. Simulated annealing: nudge and rotate one tree at a time and reject any move that causes an overlap. A worse layout is sometimes kept so the search can escape dead ends. All 200 sizes run in parallel.
  4. Greedy tree removal: if a layout with more trees fits in a smaller square, delete a tree on its edge to get a better layout for one tree fewer. Improvements cascade down to smaller sizes.
  5. Repeat the annealing and tree removal, and score every candidate before submitting.
Result
Silver Medal, top 3.3% of 3,357 teams.

Competition page on Kaggle

The key step in code

This is what makes the search simulated annealing. A better layout is always kept. A worse one is kept with probability e−Δ·1000/T, which shrinks as the "temperature" T cools, so the search explores widely at first and settles down later.

new_energy, new_real_score = energy_from(cand_env, cand_dist_sum)
delta = new_energy - current_energy

accept = False
if delta < 0:
    accept = True
else:
    if T > 1e-10:
        prob = math.exp(-delta * 1000 / T)
        accept = rng.random() < prob

Interdisciplinary Contest in Modeling: Passive Solar Shading

COMAP ICM · Problem E · 2026 · Team 2616736

Finalist: one of 26 Finalist teams out of 1,649 on Problem E. A team entry that I led. Our paper: A Lifecycle Passive Shading Model via Morphing Weather Generation and NSGA-II Optimization.

Purpose
COMAP's Interdisciplinary Contest in Modeling is an international, college-level team contest. Each team builds a mathematical model and writes a report of up to 25 pages, including a letter to the client. Our problem asked for passive solar shading, meaning shades that block hot summer sun but let warming winter sun in. We designed it for two fictional campuses, one hot and low-latitude and one cold and high-latitude, then adapted it to other climates and to a new student union that has to stay comfortable as the climate warms.
My role
My part: I led the team, chose the model's variables, compared designs wall by wall and by window-to-wall ratio, and helped write the final report.
Method
  • Simulated every hour of the year: where the sun is, how much sunlight reaches each wall, and how much of it an overhang, blinds, or trees block.
  • Fed that into a simple heat model of the building (a resistance–capacitance network, which treats heat flow like an electric circuit) to estimate heating, cooling, and summer overheating. For the cold campus, the model also tracks heat stored in exposed concrete.
  • Used a multi-objective genetic algorithm (NSGA-II) to find the best trade-offs between goals that pull against each other, such as less cooling versus less glare.
  • Compared four real cities in two pairs at similar latitudes, and re-tested designs under projected 2030 and 2050 climates from IPCC scenarios.
  • Wrote up the model and recommendations in our report, with a one-page letter to the university.
Result
Finalist, one of 26 teams out of 1,649 on Problem E. One finding from the city comparison: the best overhang depth depends on latitude. It is very shallow at about 64°N, where winter sun is scarce and worth keeping, and medium around 45 to 49°N. The project also led me to my next question: which local bus stops need shade most (see Shade Where It Matters).

Shade Where It Matters: Bus Stops near UCSB

FXB Climate Advocates · Community climate project

Ranked the shade needs of 36 transit stops around UCSB, Isla Vista, and Goleta.

Purpose
Find which bus stops most need shade, so that limited shade funding reaches the riders who depend on exposed stops in the heat. FXB Climate Advocates helps young people develop local climate projects.
My role
My own project within the program: I assessed the stops, built the scoring, and compared the rankings.
Method
  • Assessed 36 transit stops around UCSB, Isla Vista, and Goleta.
  • Scored each stop on shade and heat exposure, alongside accessibility and how much riders depend on transit.
  • Compared how the rankings changed under different weights for those factors and different budgets.
Result
A shade-priority ranking of the 36 stops, with a check of how much it depends on the weights and budget chosen. It applies the same kind of optimization as my contest work to a local public problem.

Research Paper: How Does Awe Influence Interpersonal Behavior?

Yuanpei Young Scholars Program · Advisor: Professor Shi · December 2022 – March 2023

A two-group online experiment: people shown awe-inspiring pictures rated their awe higher, but were not significantly more willing to communicate across social classes.

Purpose
Test whether feeling awe makes people more willing to communicate with people from other social classes. The hypothesis came from research on the "small self": awe can make people feel part of a larger group rather than separate individuals.
My role
Independent researcher and sole author: I built the online questionnaire, using a picture-based way of inducing awe adapted from Jiang and Sedikides (2021), screened the responses, and analyzed the data.
Method
  • An online questionnaire on the Credamo survey platform. 82 people of different ages, jobs, and social classes in China took part, and the analysis used 80 responses, 40 per group.
  • Participants were split between two groups. One group saw awe-inspiring pictures, such as large mountains and a galaxy. The other saw everyday scenes, such as a park and a shopping mall. Each person imagined being in the scene.
  • Everyone then rated, from 1 to 10, their feeling of awe and their willingness to communicate with people of a lower social class, a higher social class, and friends.
  • I removed responses finished in under 200 seconds. An open question asking people to describe a similar experience also served as a check for careless answers.
  • I compared the two groups' mean ratings and used two-sided Welch t-tests on the three willingness-to-communicate ratings.
Result
The awe group rated its awe higher than the neutral group (8.3 versus 6.2 out of 10), though I did not test that gap statistically. Willingness to communicate did not differ significantly between the groups for lower-class people, higher-class people, or friends (p = 0.59 to 0.87), so the hypothesis was not supported. An informal look at the open answers hinted at one possible effect: one awe-group answer used many group words such as "we" and "us", while many neutral-group answers described calm moments alone. I did not count or test this. I wrote the study up as an 8-page paper and presented it in a formal oral defense.

Purpose
Give reviewers a site they can skim in a minute or two on a phone or a desktop, with every claim easy to check.
My role

Method
